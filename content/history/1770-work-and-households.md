In the late 18th century, Sant Pere was one of Barcelona’s busiest manufacturing districts. Calico—printed cotton cloth with bright patterns—was becoming a major industry. A historian chose Sant Pere for a close study because most of the city’s new calico factories were clustered here.

But the story is not just “factories arrive, industry happens.” A study of 100 households in 1770 found an astonishing range of arrangements. Apprentices, servants, lodgers and unrelated workers could share a home. More than half of the sampled households included people beyond the immediate family. Sharing a roof was one way for newcomers to find a foothold and for established residents to balance the rent.

Families also stitched together different kinds of income. One study of a Barcelona cotton factory shows women and children taking on factory work alongside the master artisan’s trade; those wages could help keep a small workshop going during slow periods. Another study tracks families using small pawn loans and welfare institutions to get through hard weeks. The neighborhood’s economy ran through factories, shops, kitchens and spare beds at once.

The human scale is the hidden gem here. Census lists name neighbors, occupations, relationships and apprentices. Instead of a faceless “industrial revolution,” we meet a city where people negotiated work and care household by household.

### Pocket thought

A workshop and a home could be in the same building. Work did not stay politely at the office; the street, the stair, and the dinner table all belonged to the economy.
