If you have ever wondered why a map says **Sant Pere, Santa Caterina i la Ribera**, while a friend says “let’s meet in the Born,” you have run into Barcelona’s talent for keeping several layers of place-names alive at once.

The city’s current administrative neighborhood combines **Sant Pere, Santa Caterina and La Ribera** as one unit inside Ciutat Vella. That long name is useful: it keeps three historic identities visible instead of flattening them into one tourist-friendly label.

**Sant Pere** grew around the Benedictine women’s monastery of Sant Pere de les Puel·les, founded outside the old Roman walls in the 10th century. Medieval documents also call the quarter “La Salada.” **Santa Caterina** points to the Dominican convent whose grounds later became the market. And **La Ribera** names the broader eastern quarter whose streets once ran toward the waterfront and trading life. Archaeology shows those were real places with different centers and rhythms, even as they touched and overlapped.

So where does **Born** fit? The name is strongly associated today with the area around the old Born market and its streets. It is a familiar cultural and visitor label, but the city’s official neighborhood boundary still places MUHBA El Born inside Sant Pere, Santa Caterina i la Ribera. For a handy mental map: Born is a name used within the bigger story, not a replacement for the whole story.

That is why this guide keeps all the names in view. The short version is not “four names for the same thing”; it is “one current neighborhood containing several historical districts, plus a modern shorthand that became famous.” The borders shift with context, so ask a local what they mean and you may get a more precise answer than any map can give.

### Follow the names on foot

Start at Plaça de Sant Pere, continue toward the old Santa Caterina convent site at the market, then walk east to the Born archaeological site. You can cross the whole story in a few minutes; it took centuries to make.
