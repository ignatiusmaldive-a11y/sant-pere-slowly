A medieval neighborhood does not grow from vibes alone. Sant Pere had a practical advantage: water. The Rec Comtal, a canal that carried water from the Besòs plain toward Barcelona, ran through the eastern side of the old city. It served fields, mills and workshops for centuries.

Water could power a mill, rinse a hide, or help a craftsperson prepare materials. Archaeologists and historians have found that medieval trades were not scattered randomly: occupations that needed more water tended to cluster nearer the canal. Workshops and homes shared a tight urban fabric, while the people who owned land collected rent from the spaces where craft work happened. The district was both a place to live and a place to make things.

The Rec was not simply an ancient water pipe. It kept changing jobs as the city changed. A recent study traces how it has returned to public view through archaeological digs, neighborhood memory, urban planning and local activism. In some places it is a buried historical trace; in others, it still carries water or supports informal gardens. Infrastructure can disappear from view and keep shaping a city anyway.

Today, you can walk the old streets without seeing the canal. That is the trick of historical infrastructure: its route survives in the shape of blocks, the location of workshops, and the places where water once made work possible.

### Try this small street-name game

Look for names that hint at trades and materials. Then imagine the noise: water running, tools striking, wheels turning, neighbors sharing a wall. Sant Pere was not a museum set. It was a busy workday.
