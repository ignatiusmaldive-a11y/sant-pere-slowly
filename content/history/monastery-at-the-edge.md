Before Sant Pere was a neighborhood label, it was the name of a monastery. In 945, Count Sunyer and Countess Riquilda founded a Benedictine house for women beyond Barcelona’s then-walled city. That “beyond the walls” detail matters: the old town grew outward, and the monastery was one of the anchors that made this patch of edge feel like somewhere.

Think of the monastery less as a quiet building on its own and more as a landlord, employer, religious center and archive. Over time, land around it was leased and settled. Houses, workshops, gardens and lanes gathered nearby. The monastery’s name travelled from the institution to the growing quarter around it. The map kept the name long after the city swallowed the old edge.

And the women were not just a footnote. A study of the monastery’s records follows Abbess Violant d’Espés on a pastoral visit in 1585–1587. That kind of visit was a practical inspection of the community and its religious life: a glimpse of a woman exercising authority in a city whose institutions were often run by men.

The church you can visit today has been rebuilt and altered across many centuries, including after serious damage in 985 and later fires and restorations. It is not a perfectly preserved time capsule. It is more interesting than that: a building whose scars show how often Barcelona has had to make, remake and reuse its past.

### A good detail to notice

Look for the church’s compact, almost fortress-like presence at Plaça de Sant Pere. It sits in a busy neighborhood, but its name still carries the memory of an institution that once stood beyond the city.
